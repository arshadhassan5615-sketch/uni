## 1. The map (0:00)
*Black screen. The Amazon basin fades in as one still map: tree cover in muted green, forest loss 2001-2024 in the yellow-to-red ramp.*

> This is the Amazon basin, seen from orbit. The coloured pixels are places where a satellite recorded that forest was lost between two thousand and one and two thousand and twenty-four. [15]
> Each pixel covers about thirty metres of ground. [15]
> Today we will use maps like this one to ask a simple question. How much forest is being lost, and how do we know?

## 2. What is at stake (0:40)
*Globe. Number 4.14 billion ha counts up once. Second number 1.18 billion ha.*

> Forests cover four point one four billion hectares. About one third of the land on Earth. [1]
> At least one point one eight billion hectares of that is classified as primary forest. [4]
> These figures come from the Food and Agriculture Organization's Global Forest Resources Assessment, published in October two thousand and twenty-five. It covers two hundred and thirty-six countries and areas. [1]

## 3. How a satellite sees a forest (1:15)
*Timeline 1972 to today. Landsat and Sentinel-2 cards. Then a pixel grid zooming into one 30 m cell.*

> Mapping forests from space is not new. NASA's first Landsat satellite launched on the twenty-third of July, nineteen seventy-two. Since then, Landsat satellites have continuously acquired images of the Earth's land surface. [13]
> Today Europe's Sentinel-2 satellites add images at ten-metre resolution, revisiting the same ground about every five days. [14]
> A map is only as good as its definition. In the global forest change dataset used by Global Forest Watch, tree cover means vegetation taller than five metres. Loss means a stand-replacement disturbance, a change from forest to non-forest. [15]
> That definition matters. It counts what satellites can see, which is not always the same as what a country calls deforestation.

## 4. Three systems, three questions (2:10)
*Three columns appear on the map's side: FAO (country reports), Global Forest Watch (satellite tree cover loss), INPE PRODES (Brazilian Amazon only). Same map stays behind.*

> There is no single number for deforestation, because there is no single question.
> The FAO collects reports from countries, and measures change in land use. Global Forest Watch measures tree cover loss from satellites, which includes loss from fire. And Brazil's space agency, INPE, runs a programme called PRODES that measures clearing in the Brazilian Legal Amazon only.
> Those numbers should never be placed on one axis as if they measured the same thing. In this film, each one will be labelled with its source.

## 5. The global trend, according to FAO (3:00)
*Bars: deforestation 17.6 (1990-2000) and 10.9 (2015-2025) million ha per year; net loss 10.7 and 4.12.*

> Here is the good news, and it is real. According to the FAO, deforestation slowed to ten point nine million hectares per year between two thousand fifteen and twenty twenty-five. In the nineteen nineties it was seventeen point six. [2]
> Net forest loss, which subtracts forest that grows back or is planted, fell from ten point seven million hectares per year to four point one two. [3]
> But ten point nine million hectares a year is still a very large number. The trend is falling. The loss has not stopped.

## 6. Zooming in: the Amazon (3:50)
*Camera pushes from world to region (arc of deforestation). Hansen loss year layer animates 2001 -> 2024, year counter in the corner.*

> Now the camera moves closer. This is the Brazilian Amazon, and the colours are years. Yellow is early. Red is recent.
> Every shade on this map was recorded in a dataset that anyone can download. [15]
> INPE publishes the same kind of detail for the Brazilian Legal Amazon. Its yearly deforestation layer contains more than eight hundred thousand individual polygons, each with a date and an area. [10]

## 7. What INPE measured (4:40)
*Detail altitude. Two numbers on the map: 6,288 and 5,796 -> 5,731 km2.*

> For the year from August two thousand twenty-three to July two thousand twenty-four, INPE estimated six thousand two hundred and eighty-eight square kilometres of deforestation. [8]
> For the following year, the first estimate was five thousand seven hundred and ninety-six square kilometres. After the data were consolidated in March two thousand twenty-six, the figure became five thousand seven hundred and thirty-one. [9]
> Notice that detail. Even official numbers are revised, and a careful presentation says which version it is using.

## 8. The fire year (5:25)
*World altitude. Tropical primary forest loss: 6.7 million ha in 2024, 4.3 million ha in 2025.*

> Satellites also show that the story is not only about clearing. Global Forest Watch reported that in two thousand twenty-four, the world lost six point seven million hectares of tropical primary forest, nearly twice as much as in two thousand twenty-three. For the first time on record, fires, not agriculture, were the leading cause, at nearly fifty percent. [11]
> Brazil accounted for forty-two percent of that loss, and fires caused sixty-six percent of Brazil's. [12]
> In two thousand twenty-five, tropical primary forest loss fell by thirty-six percent, to four point three million hectares. That is still forty-six percent higher than a decade earlier. [5]
> Across all kinds of tree cover, not only primary forest, the world lost about thirty million hectares in two thousand twenty-four, five percent more than the year before. [12]
> In two thousand twenty-five, global tree cover loss declined by fourteen percent, to twenty-five point five million hectares. Fires were responsible for forty-two percent of it. [6]
> That is an area larger than the United Kingdom. [6]

## 9. What the maps tell us (6:10)
*Return to the opening map, all layers visible. Spotlight on one sentence at a time.*

> What do these maps tell us? That deforestation is measurable, in near real time, and in public.
> They also tell us the target is not met. Global Forest Watch reports that deforestation in two thousand twenty-five was seventy percent higher than the level needed to halt and reverse forest loss by two thousand thirty. [7]
> A map cannot protect a forest. But it can show us, pixel by pixel, whether we are keeping our promises.
